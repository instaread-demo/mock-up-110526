Everest Base Camp via Gokyo Lakes, Cho La and Kongma La: My Trekking Guide

November 2, 2026 • 0 Comment

The Everest region is breathtaking, but planning an Everest Base Camp trek can be overwhelming. There are several routes, high passes and viewpoints to choose from, and you need enough time for acclimatisation and unpredictable mountain flights. I decided to experience Everest Base Camp together with Gokyo Lakes and the quieter Kongma La crossing not to just cross out the Everest Base camp from a to-do-list but to experience even the less visited Everest areas. I recommend 3-4 weeks in Nepal, including the trek, arrival preparations and several buffer days after returning from Lukla. I explored this route in October 2022 with my wife, parents and friends, and created this guide to help you plan your own journey step by step, including the best time to go, flights to Lukla, guides, permits and accommodation.

Below, I share both the route I would recommend and what our own trek was really like. Start with the overview, map and planning advice, then follow the recommended itinerary and read our day-by-day story including distances, elevation gain, my favourite viewpoints and many other details I found interesting on the way. Our 2022 trek included Pokalde and a rushed descent, so the recommended circuit allows more time for acclimatisation and a gentler return. I finish with practical advice on guides, permits, teahouses, food and packing.

My recommended Everest Base Camp circuit at a glance

This is the circuit I would recommend after our October 2022 trek, keeping the acclimatisation day we missed and taking a slower return through Chukhung, Phortse and Monjo. We also wanted to climb a mountain on our adventure and we decided to ascend a hidden gem mountain Pokalde on the way. It is an optional one-night extension.

My recommended circuit: Lukla → Phakding → Namche Bazaar → Dole → Machhermo → Gokyo and Gokyo Ri → Thangnak → Cho La → Dzongla → Lobuche → Everest Base Camp/Gorakshep → Kala Patthar → Lobuche → Kongma La → Optional Pokalde climb → Chukhung → Phortse → Monjo → Lukla.

If you want to add a climb to Pokalde you will sleep at the lakeside high camp just under the Kongma La pass. Climb Pokalde the next morning, return to camp and continue to Chukhung. Then, follow the same Phortse → Monjo → Lukla return afterwards. This adds one camp night and one day.

Pro tip: I would also keep one flexible mountain day for either version. It might not be necessary but if your flight is delayed like ours, you’ll be very thankful and you won’t regret anyway one more day in the Himalayas.

The recommended day-by-day itinerary is below. My personal account then shows the route we actually walked in October 2022.

When is the best time to trek via Gokyo and Cho La?

Next time, I would choose the first half of November. We did this trek in October, and the weather caused problems before we even reached the mountains. There was a bad weather forecast for our departure date so we decided to fly one day earlier. But it turned out to be bad weather even that day so we got stranded at Ramechhap airport for two nights. Later, we also had cloudy, rainy or snowy mornings during the trek.

I would accept the colder mornings and nights of early November in exchange for the prospect of more settled post-monsoon weather. That is my preference for a return trip, rather than a promise of clear skies. The Nepal Tourism Board identifies spring and autumn as the main trekking seasons and highlights the often-clear skies in October and November after the monsoon but the November brings statistically even drier but also colder days than October. Before booking your trip, compare average precipitation and temperature at Namche Bazaar not to be surprised by some rain/snow showers or lower temperatures.

Spring is another option to investigate, especially if seeing the Everest expedition season is part of the attraction. Whatever month you choose, check recent snow and the condition of the high passes before committing to a crossing.

My most useful photography tip applies throughout the trip: go outside before breakfast. Don’t miss a sunrise! Even when the village is still in shadow, the summits above it can already be glowing orange and red. We had some wonderful moments when the light and weather came together. A cloudy forecast would not stop me checking outside before deciding to stay in my sleeping bag. The same applies to sunsets. Plan your dinner before or after not to miss a potential fantastic scenery.

For Pokalde, the condition of the climbing route matters as well as the general trekking season. Our approach had snow and ice, while the sunnier side of the mountain was completely snow free.

How many days do you need to visit Everest Base Camp?

The number of days depends on which Everest Base Camp route you choose. The direct route is the shortest of the four options below. Gokyo adds lakes and fantastic Gokyo Ri viewpoints, a glacier crossing and beautiful Cho La pass; Kongma La adds another beautiful high crossing and a feeling that you are alone in the Everest area. The full Three Passes Trek also includes the western valley and Renjo La.

These are planning durations from the flight into Lukla to the planned flight out, including acclimatisation days. Add arrival and preparation time, plus several Kathmandu buffer days afterwards. These are not guaranteed completion times.

For comparison, Seven Summit Treks’ classic itinerary uses 12 days from the flight into Lukla to the flight out, within a longer Nepal programme. Longer routes need their own schedule; do not compare package lengths without checking which Kathmandu days they include. I strongly recommend rather longer itinerary with proper acclimatization than rushing.

Why I was so happy with Gokyo and Kongma La

I was extremely happy with our decision. The main Everest trail was busy, while Gokyo felt quieter and Kongma La gave us the quietest and most beautiful part of our journey. We had lakes, glacier views, different valleys and eventually a camp below the pass with nobody else around. Well, there was something – one of the most beautiful sunsets and milky ways in my life!

I understand the appeal of the full Three Passes Trek. For us, this circuit was enough. By the end, we were happy to be heading home, and adding Renjo La and another valley would probably have been one extension too many.

Keep an extra mountain day for Namche or Phortse

I would keep the planned acclimatisation day in Namche and add one more flexible day to the mountain itinerary. If we needed more time at the beginning, I would use it for even three nights in Namche or adjust another overnight stop with our guide for better acclimatization, with the walking adjusted to how everyone felt. If the ascent went well, I would use it later in Phortse.

We lost a day to bad weather at Ramechhap and tried to recover it by skipping our second night in Namche Bazaar. My recordings show us reaching Namche on 7 October and continuing to Dole on 8 October, without the day intended for an acclimatisation hike towards the Everest View Hotel. Both my parents later had difficult moments at altitude. It’s very likely because of the worse acclimatization. Missing the Namche acclimatisation day may have contributed.

Our original programme already included a return through Phortse. My parents went there and loved the village, its local Buddhist culture, its gompa (Buddhist monastery). Joanne and I skipped that stop and descended faster because we were looking forward to be back in civilization. That was a mistake: the rushed descent hurt her legs badly that Joanne had to go backwards, and we missed a place we would have enjoyed.

Next time, I would keep Phortse in the return route and, if the extra day was still available, stay another night. Nepal Tourism Board also highlights Phortse as a Sherpa village to visit on the return from Everest Base Camp.

Put your Kathmandu sightseeing days after the trek

Once in Nepal, it’s worth to see Kathmandu as well. Keep several flexible days in Kathmandu after returning from the mountains. If the flights to and from Lukla run to plan, use them for sightseeing and a comfortable finish. If the trek or Lukla flight is delayed, those are the days you can give up.

We were very glad to have some flexibility at the end, but I would leave more next time. Joanne and I arrived before my parents, who joined us two days later, so we had more spare time at the beginning than we really needed. Near the end, we were stressed about getting back for our flight home.

Missing a Kathmandu sightseeing day is disappointing. Missing your international flight home is a much bigger problem. I would put the spare time where it can help with the return.

How do you get to Lukla from Kathmandu?

We arrived in Kathmandu on 1 October 2022, but our Lukla flight departed from Ramechhap. Most scheduled Lukla services had been shifted there that October to reduce congestion at Kathmandu Airport. This was unfortunate because our flights were getting cancelled and we were stranded for two nights at Ramechhap. Luckily, we didn’t have to sleep on the airport floor. Our guide fixed an accommodation for us in the village.

Can you fly directly from Kathmandu to Lukla?

Yes, direct Kathmandu Lukla flights are offered now, depending on the dates and operating arrangements. Tara Air’s current guidance describes both Kathmandu–Lukla and Ramechhap–Lukla flights, with some services using Ramechhap during busy periods. Check the actual departure airport for your dates, including the return flight.

If a direct Kathmandu flight is available, I would definitely choose it. The taxi journey to Ramechhap was horrible, and I would happily avoid it on a return visit. A Kathmandu departure removes that road transfer, although the mountain flight itself can still be delayed by weather.

What if your flight leaves from Ramechhap?

Ramechhap Airport is also called Manthali Airport. You need a road transfer from Kathmandu, so have the trekking company confirm the pickup, any overnight accommodation and what happens if the flight is postponed. Check the airport codes: KTM–LUA is Kathmandu–Lukla; RHP–LUA is Ramechhap–Lukla.

The flight itself was a memorable introduction to the Himalayas. I am not the most relaxed flyer so I was very stressed before but the flight was actually smooth in the end, and arriving at Lukla was epic. The views in general made it one of the most beautiful flights I have taken.

My practical advice is straightforward:

  • Keep several days between your planned return from Lukla and an essential international connection.
  • Check the airline’s baggage allowance separately from your trekking company’s porter allowance.
  • Agree in advance who handles rebooking, additional nights and changes to the road transfer.
  • If flights are delayed, discuss which optional parts of the itinerary can be removed while preserving acclimatisation.

What itinerary would I recommend?

I was so happy on our trip that I recommend it to everyone booking Everest base camp experience. I would plan one trekking circuit and add Pokalde as an optional one-night extension. The whole trek covers the highlights Gokyo Ri, Cho La, Dzongla, Kala Patthar, Everest Base Camp and Kongma La. On the way back to Lukla, I recommend staying in Chukhung, Phortse, Monjo and Lukla.

This is the itinerary I would choose for a return visit. It restores the Namche acclimatisation day we missed and changes the final stages from our actual, hurried descent. My parents followed their own slower return and visited Phortse; this table is a recommendation for the full Kongma La circuit, rather than a diary of their route.

Numbering starts with the flight into Lukla. The base schedule takes 17 days, including the planned flight out. Add a day for Pokalde and keep a further flexible mountain day for either version. To make the timetable even safer, keep space for arrival and preparation time beforehand, and several Kathmandu buffer days afterwards. Our guide was repeating very often advice: “Slowly, slowly” and that’s really how the Himalayas work. Hurrying doesn’t make sense here.

The trekking itinerary without Pokalde

Namche to Dole is a substantial increase in sleeping altitude. That’s why I strongly recommend to have at least one extra acclimatization day at Namche. Discuss a slower ascent with your guide and allow additional nights if needed. This itinerary isn’t a fixed schedule to follow if you have altitude sickness symptoms. Remember: Slowly, slowly.

Chukhung to Phortse is still a full walking day. If you need shorter stages, split it with a night in Dingboche rather than forcing the descent. Keeping Phortse and Monjo gives the return more time than the two long days Joanne and I recorded from Dingboche to Lukla.

Where would I put the extra flexible day?

Keep one floating mountain day in addition to either schedule. If more acclimatisation is needed early on, use it in Namche, giving three nights there, or adjust the overnight stops with your guide. That moves the later stages back by a day. If it is still available on the return, I would spend a second night in Phortse, visit the gompa and enjoy the village without rushing.

Alternatively, use the extra day if there is a bad weather forecast on a day when you are planning to go to one of the viewpoints on the way. You don’t want to miss a view over any of the 8000m mountains on the way.

That gives 18 days without Pokalde or 19 with Pokalde, including the Lukla flights and one flexible mountain day. Extra weather days or a further stop in Dingboche need additional time. Keep the Kathmandu sightseeing buffer separately as well: the mountain day gives flexibility during the trek, and the Kathmandu days give room before your international flight.

Which 8,000-metre peaks can you see along the way?

With clear skies, you can see four 8,000-metre peaks on this circuit: Everest, Lhotse, Makalu and Cho Oyu. What were the must-see viewpoints?

  • Gokyo Ri is the standout viewpoint for seeing all four in one panorama.
  • You’ll be able to see Cho Oyu from the upper Gokyo valley. We enjoyed the views especially on a viewpoint close to Thangnak.
  • Everest isn’t continuously visible along the trail. Kala Patthar gave us my favourite close view of its summit
  • Kongma La crossing offers the best views of Lhotse and Makalu. We enjoyed especially view over Makalu from our lakeside camp below the pass during the sunset, and the optional Pokalde summit provides even better view of it.

Our trek to Everest Base Camp via Gokyo, Cho La and Kongma La: what was each section like?

Before I give you detailed description of our trek, I want to add a note on how I measured the distance and elevation: I recorded the stages below with my Suunto watch. ↑ means cumulative ascent and ↓ means cumulative descent, including the smaller climbs and dips along the way, rather than just the difference in height between two villages. My watch recording can pick up small ups and downs that a modelled Mapy elevation profile doesn’t cover. That’s why the elevation meters differ. The routes also differ slightly: my recordings include Pokalde and our shorter return, while the map above shows the circuit I recommend now. Sensor noise and calibration can affect watch readings too, so treat these as recorded estimates.

My watch recordings cover all stages from Lukla to Everest Base Camp, Pokalde and back from 6–20 October 2022 and altogether, it measured total 144.2 km, with approximately 10,248 m of recorded ascent and 9,259 m of recorded descent, including Pokalde. The dates and distances in this diary describe our actual journey, which differs slightly from the recommended versions above.

Our recorded circuit: Lukla → Phakding → Namche Bazaar → Dole → Machhermo → Gokyo and Gokyo Ri → Thangnak → Cho La → Dzongla → Lobuche → Everest Base Camp/Gorakshep → Kala Patthar → Lobuche → Kongma La and lakeside camp → Pokalde → Dingboche → Pangboche → Tengboche → Namche → Lukla.

We skipped the Namche acclimatisation day and returned directly from the high camp towards Dingboche, bypassing Chukhung. We then hurried back through Tengboche and Namche. Next time, I would do it slower with stays in Chukhung, Phortse and Monjo.


Day 1: Fly to Lukla and walk to Phakding (6 October 2022)

  • Length: 8.9 km recorded.
  • Elevation up: 307 m.
  • Elevation down: 467 m.
  • Destination altitude: Lukla around 2,860 m; Phakding around 2,610 m overnight.
  • Highlight: Sherpa Brewery craft beer and watching planes at Lukla Airport.

Once we landed in Lukla, everything felt better. The stress of waiting for the weather and worrying about the flight was behind us. Now we could enjoy being in the Himalayas.

Temba introduced a lesson we would hear many more times: “Slowly, slowly.” There was no reason to hurry anymore. We celebrated our arrival with a craft beer from Sherpa Brewery and watched other planes landing and taking off. Lukla Airport is fascinating when you are safely on the ground watching someone else use it.

When we finally set off, the walking was a gentle introduction to the trek, with more descent than climbing. We followed the valley through cute villages, and we were learning how to pass or get passed by a lot of yaks carrying goods for higher villages.

This was a good day to bear in mind Temba’s advice. Taking our time allowed us to observe the fantastic life around Lukla and it was a good practice for gaining altitude next days. The beer was our little arrival celebration; the slow walking pace was the lesson to remember.

Day 2: Phakding to Namche Bazaar (7 October 2022)

  • Length: 11.4 km recorded.
  • Elevation up: 978 m.
  • Elevation down: 327 m.
  • Destination altitude: Namche Bazaar around 3,440 m overnight.
  • Highlight: Suspension bridges above the river and arriving in Namche.

The day began gently, following the river through a forested canyon. Then the suspension bridges came, hanging high above the water, with prayer flags adding colour to the crossing. They were among my favourite features of these first days.

The final climb to Namche was considerably steeper. This was the first time the altitude made an ordinary uphill walk feel like a much bigger effort. Slowly, slowly suddenly seemed especially sensible.

Namche itself was beautiful: a town climbing around the mountainside, with more choice in food and accommodation than we would find higher up. I tried a yak burger, visited an Irish bar for a drink and walked up to a viewpoint. Across the valley, the snowy Kongde Ri and Nupla range gave us our first memorable mountain backdrop. This was the last place we felt like in a civilization.

We also stopped to watch children playing volleyball. This was far from the last time we saw locals playing it, and they were impressively good. We had come expecting mountains; the volleyball was an unexpected recurring attraction.

Our hotel gave us one more comfortable, warm night. I appreciated it even more once we reached the colder lodges farther up the valley.

Day 3: Namche Bazaar to Dole (8 October 2022)

  • Length: 12.6 km recorded.
  • Elevation up: 1,174 m.
  • Elevation down: 488 m.
  • Destination altitude: Dole around 4,200 m overnight.
  • Highlight: A brief, beautiful appearance of Ama Dablam through the clouds.

This was a long day, with more than 1,000 metres of recorded climbing. We had left Namche without the acclimatisation day originally planned there, trying to recover the day lost to our delayed flight. I would keep that extra Namche night next time.

The scenery helped with the effort. The route towards the Gokyo valley brings wonderful mountain views, although clouds hid much of them during our walk. Ama Dablam kept us waiting, then showed part of itself for a few minutes, covered in fresh snow. That brief appearance was enough to make me stop and stare. It might be the most beautiful mountain in the whole region.

Cloudy days have their own atmosphere, but they also teach you patience. You keep looking up, hoping the next opening will reveal something. The little glimpse of Ama Dablam made the climbing feel worthwhile.

By Dole, we were properly in the high mountains. The accommodation was simple, and having a private room didn’t mean having a warm room. I was very happy we brought warm sleeping bags.

Day 4: Dole to Machhermo (9 October 2022)

  • Length: 5.3 km recorded.
  • Elevation up: 679 m.
  • Elevation down: 275 m.
  • Destination altitude: Machhermo around 4,470 m overnight.
  • Highlight: A clear sunrise and seeing yak dung drying for fuel.

We had our first really beautiful sunrise, with mountain views and a little morning yoga. These are the moments I came to the Himalayas for. Getting outside early before breakfast sounds uncomfortable but the views are so worth it.

The walk to Machhermo was shorter than the previous day’s stage, but the altitude was making itself felt. A short distance on the map could still require plenty of effort. Temba’s “Slowly, slowly” was becoming the unofficial slogan of the trip.

One of the most interesting sights was much closer to the ground: yak dung laid out to dry, ready to be used as fuel later. Watching everyday mountain life was as much a part of the journey as photographing the peaks. After spending a cold night in a lodge, you gain a new appreciation for anything that can fuel a stove.

I also managed to get stung on the neck by a bee while hurrying along. Of all the encounters I had imagined having in the Himalayas, that had not been high on the list. Another reason to remember the slow pace.

Day 5: Machhermo to Gokyo Lakes (10 October 2022)

  • Length: 7.3 km recorded.
  • Elevation up: 384 m.
  • Elevation down: 50 m.
  • Destination altitude: Gokyo around 4,790 m overnight.
  • Highlight: Turquoise Gokyo Lakes and a kettle heated by a solar cooker.

Clouds accompanied us again as we walked towards Gokyo, but the lakes gave the landscape a wonderful atmosphere. Even without a complete mountain panorama, the water and the shapes of the surrounding slopes made this feel very different from the lower valley.

In the afternoon, the sun finally came out and we could enjoy the beautiful turquoise water properly. The changing light transformed the scene. This was exactly the variety I had hoped for when we chose to include the Gokyo valley.

There was another interesting sight outside a lodge: a kettle sitting above an enormous reflective dish. It was a parabolic solar cooker. The curved reflector concentrates sunlight onto the kettle, heating the water directly rather than generating electricity. No power socket required and the water was boiling!

Between the lake, the mountains and this ingenious use of sunshine, Gokyo offered a lot to enjoy without going very far. We would stay another night and use the following morning for Gokyo Ri.

Day 6: Gokyo Ri and back to Gokyo (11 October 2022)

  • Length: 4.0 km recorded.
  • Elevation up: 620 m.
  • Elevation down: 583 m.
  • Destination altitude: Gokyo Ri 5,357 m; back to Gokyo around 4,790 m overnight.
  • Highlight: Our first broad panorama of the peaks, lakes and glacier.

Gokyo Ri became one of the highlights of the whole trip. We set off early and worked our way up above the village, taking the climb slowly. Once we were high enough, the view opened out over the lakes, the glacier and the mountains surrounding us.

There were still clouds moving around the peaks. Everest and Cho Oyu remained partly hidden, making us wait for the clearer view we had been hoping for. Even so, this was our first broad mountain panorama, and the combination of water, ice and rock below us was extraordinary.

The Ngozumpa Glacier particularly surprised me. Much of it was covered in debris, so it looked very different from the clean blue ice people often imagine. From above, its enormous scale became much easier to appreciate.

My mum struggled with her breathing during the climb. It was a reminder that a viewpoint hike at this altitude is a serious outing. I wish we had allowed more time for acclimatisation earlier in the trip. We returned to the same lodge in Gokyo, but this was far more than a simple rest-day stroll.

Day 7: Cross the Ngozumpa Glacier to Thangnak (12 October 2022)

  • Length: 3.8 km recorded.
  • Elevation up: 182 m.
  • Elevation down: 286 m.
  • Destination altitude: Thangnak, also called Dragnag, around 4,700 m overnight; the sunset lookout was above the village.
  • Highlight: Cho Oyu finally revealing itself during our sunset walk.

We woke to a snow shower. After the views from Gokyo Ri, it was quite a change to start the next day in snow and fog.

The glacier crossing was atmospheric rather than panoramic. We walked through a rugged landscape of moraine, debris and ice, with much of the surrounding mountain scenery hidden. I began to wonder whether Cho Oyu would remain behind a cloud for the whole visit.

Fortunately, the weather improved after we reached Thangnak, also written as Dragnag or Thagnak. We postponed dinner and walked to a small nearby hill instead. That decision gave us a wonderful evening above the glacier, with Cho Oyu finally visible in the sunset light and other majestic mountains around it.

This was one of the rewards for staying flexible. The main walk had been cloudy and snowy, but we still ended the day with a view I had been waiting for. Sometimes dinner can wait.

Day 8: Cho La Pass and a night in Dzongla (13 October 2022)

  • Length: 9.5 km recorded.
  • Elevation up: 1,168 m.
  • Elevation down: 696 m.
  • Destination altitude: Cho La around 5,420 m; Dzongla around 4,830 m overnight.
  • Highlight: The pass panorama, followed by sunset beneath Ama Dablam and Cholatse.

There was fresh snow again in the morning, but this time it came with a clear sky. The mountains looked wonderful, and we had the weather we had hoped for on the pass day.

The ascent didn’t feel especially technical to me in those conditions. We put on microspikes for the snowy and icy ground, and there were ropes to help on parts of our crossing. Those were the conditions and facilities we found in 2022; check the current state of the route with your guide.

The views were beautiful as we climbed and became even better at the top, where Ama Dablam was a wonderful part of the panorama. We didn’t stay for long, though. My father was struggling this time, and getting down took priority over another round of photographs.

Dzongla provided a second highlight. With Ama Dablam and Cholatse around us, we enjoyed a sunset photo session in one of the best placed villages I’ve seen in my life. Cholatse quickly became another contender for my favourite mountain.

I was very glad we slept here rather than trying to continue to Lobuche. After a demanding crossing, this small village deserved time to enjoy.

Day 9: Dzongla to Lobuche (14 October 2022)

  • Length: 6.6 km recorded.
  • Elevation up: 386 m.
  • Elevation down: 230 m.
  • Destination altitude: Lobuche around 4,940 m overnight.
  • Highlight: Sunrise coffee and the Cholatse–Taboche panorama above a turquoise lake.

I had the best morning coffee time of my life in Dzongla. A cup in my hand, sunrise on the mountains and those enormous slopes around us: the setting was difficult to beat.

This was another reason to keep an overnight stop here. If you simply rush through a village, you miss the changing light and the quiet moments before the day’s walkers arrive. The morning was every bit as rewarding as the sunset had been.

On the walk towards Lobuche, Cholatse and Taboche formed a wonderful mountain panorama, with a turquoise lake beneath them. This is also a panoramic view my parents decided to print above their couch. Now, it belongs to our family memories forever.

The stage was relatively short, which gave us a welcome change after Cho La. We spent the night in Lobuche instead of combining this walk with the pass crossing. It also put us in position for the following day’s journey to Everest Base Camp.

Day 10: Everest Base Camp and overnight in Gorakshep (15 October 2022)

  • Length: 11.1 km recorded.
  • Elevation up: 607 m.
  • Elevation down: 317 m.
  • Destination altitude: Everest Base Camp around 5,364 m; Gorakshep around 5,170 m overnight.
  • Highlight: Nuptse and the glacier landscape around Everest Base Camp.

We walked from Lobuche to Gorakshep, left our luggage there and continued towards Everest Base Camp before returning to Gorakshep to sleep. At this altitude, the effort was becoming a problem for some members of our group. Some only walked part of the way before turning back to rest.

Reaching the famous destination was satisfying, and we took the photograph that almost everyone wants after walking this far. My visual highlights, though, were Nuptse and the glacier landscape rather than the Base Camp marker itself.

We visited the Everest area in autumn, so there was no large expedition tent city of the sort associated with the spring climbing season. There was basically nothing happening at Base Camp but the journey there was fantastic.

Gorakshep was our highest teahouse overnight. It was also the coldest and the helicopter traffic was noticeable, and after the busier Everest section I was already looking forward to a quieter part of the route. First, however, there was one more viewpoint I really wanted to reach.

Day 11: Kala Patthar at sunrise, then back to Lobuche (16 October 2022)

  • Length: 8.8 km recorded.
  • Elevation up: 608 m.
  • Elevation down: 742 m.
  • Destination altitude: Kala Patthar roughly 5,600 m; Lobuche around 4,940 m overnight.
  • Highlight: The Everest panorama from Kala Patthar at sunrise.

We woke up early for Kala Patthar, the highest viewpoint on this circuit unless you add Pokalde. At this altitude, the climb can be a serious effort, especially if you haven’t acclimatised well. We followed the familiar “Slowly, slowly” approach and reached the viewpoint in time for sunrise.

This was the Everest view I had been waiting for. The panorama was fantastic in every direction, and standing up there felt far more memorable to me than posing beside the Base Camp marker the previous day.

We had chosen the morning because the previous afternoon had been cloudy. I would still consider sunset on a return visit with suitable conditions, but I was very happy we had got out of bed early enough for this experience.

My parents rested instead of joining the climb. I wished they could have seen it too, but they needed that recovery time for the remaining days.

My watch recorded 4.2 km for the Kala Patthar round trip and another 4.6 km from Gorakshep back to Lobuche. We slept in Lobuche again before the group divided for the next part of the journey.

Day 12: Kongma La and our lakeside high camp (17 October 2022)

  • Length: 5.1 km recorded.
  • Elevation up: 732 m.
  • Elevation down: 231 m.
  • Destination altitude: Kongma La around 5,535 m; lakeside camp approximately 5,430 m overnight, estimated from the watch.
  • Highlight: A completely quiet camp, Makalu at sunset and the Milky Way above Pokalde.

Sunrise and a clear sky promised another wonderful day. Joanne and I said goodbye to the rest of our group, who started their lower return towards Lukla via Phortse. We headed towards the biggest adventure of our trip with our guide and one porter.

The day began with a glacier crossing, followed by a steep ascent to Kongma La. I have praised this detour many times, and I will do it again: it was beautiful, with views of Lhotse and Makalu, and we met no other trekkers during our crossing.

The best part was still ahead. Below the pass, our guide and porter set up camp beside a lake. There were only four of us in the whole setting. We enjoyed dinner outside in beautiful weather, watched an unforgettable sunset and looked out towards Makalu. It felt insane. We felt like mountaineers waiting for the first ascent of some undiscovered peak.

Our porter knew the Pokalde line best and showed our guide the route that afternoon. He would stay at camp while the three of us climbed the following morning.

After dark, even a trip to the toilet became a highlight. I was delighted to be awake when I saw the Milky Way aligned above Pokalde. For me, the sunset, night sky and complete quiet made this the best experience of the entire journey.

Day 13: Climb Pokalde and descend to Dingboche (18 October 2022)

  • Length: 9.7 km recorded.
  • Elevation up: 830 m.
  • Elevation down: 1,659 m.
  • Destination altitude: Pokalde 5,806 m; return via the high camp at approximately 5,430 m; Dingboche around 4,410 m overnight.
  • Highlight: A clear, exposed summit panorama with no other climbers around.

The morning was very cold. We climbed in shadow, with snow and ice underfoot, but the effort soon warmed us up. Above us was a clear sky, and there was almost no wind.

We had crampons, helmets, harnesses and a rope, with trekking poles useful on the approach. One steep passage required rope protection. The final section was very steep and extremely exposed, but the summit rewarded us with a wonderful view in every direction. We had it completely to ourselves.

It was just Joanne, our guide and me on the mountain. I would climb Pokalde again without hesitation. It felt like a hidden gem compared with the busier objectives around us. I know many peaks around are above 6000 m and it’s tempting to reach this elevation milestone but I would definitely trade it for this hidden gem. The summit round trip was only ca 2.6 km with 694 m of recorded ascent but remember, it wasn’t a piece of cake. Climbing at these altitudes and temperatures requires proper acclimatization and physical preparation.

After descending to camp, we realised we still had time left in the day and started thinking about getting home sooner. We decided to save a day by skipping Chukhung. Our guide knew a shortcut towards Dingboche, and that is where we spent the night after a further 7.1 km of walking.

It seemed like a useful decision then. The effect of compressing the return became much clearer in the following two days. Joanne won’t be thankful for this decision.

Day 14: Dingboche through Tengboche to Namche Bazaar (19 October 2022)

  • Length: 20.7 km recorded.
  • Elevation up: 853 m.
  • Elevation down: 1,577 m.
  • Destination altitude: Tengboche around 3,860 m on the way; Namche Bazaar around 3,440 m overnight.
  • Highlight: Looking back towards Lhotse and finally seeing Ama Dablam clearly.

We had a beautifully clear day for the return. I kept turning around to look back towards Lhotse and find another composition for a photograph. Ama Dablam was also finally visible in its full beauty, giving us the views that clouds had hidden earlier in the trek.

Our route went through Pangboche and Tengboche before reaching Namche. The scenery was wonderful, but it was also a long day after the climb and all the walking that had come before. Even this mainly descending stage included plenty of uphill effort.

My parents had kept the Phortse visit and loved the village, its Buddhist culture and its gompa. Joanne and I took this faster return instead. I wish we had given ourselves the time for that slower route too. By the time we arrived at a comfortable hotel with a shower in Namche, Joanne’s legs were so sore that even the stairs were a problem. We still had another full day to reach Lukla. I was beginning to worry about how she would manage it.

Don’t underestimate the descent. Gaining less altitude doesn’t mean your legs get an easy day.

Day 15: Namche Bazaar to Lukla (20 October 2022)

  • Length: 19.3 km recorded.
  • Elevation up: 741 m.
  • Elevation down: 1,332 m.
  • Destination altitude: Via Phakding around 2,610 m; Lukla around 2,860 m overnight.
  • Highlight: Reaching Lukla together after a very difficult descent for Joanne.

Joanne’s legs were already very painful in the morning. The initial steep descent from Namche quickly showed why shortening the return had been a bad decision.

She reached a point where she could barely walk downhill normally and had to turn around and descend backwards. I felt terrible watching her struggle. I even started thinking about finding a yak to carry her down, but she wanted to continue.

Fortunately, things improved once we were past the steepest ground. At the end, she was actually pleased when the route began climbing again towards Lukla after Phakding. It was an unusual feeling to welcome the uphill sections at the end of a long trek, but on that day they were much easier for her to manage.

We eventually reached Lukla together. After everything we had seen, from the Gokyo Lakes to the quiet Pokalde camp, this final arrival felt like its own achievement. Another Sherpa Craft beer and proper shower were other wonderful rewards.

Our final two days added up to about 40 km. Next time, I would keep the Chukhung, Phortse and Monjo overnight stops in the recommended itinerary above and give the descent more time. Walking backwards is a very specific memory from our trip, not a technique I would suggest relying on.

How should you think about acclimatisation on the Everest Base Camp trek?

Our missed Namche day is the decision I would most clearly change. Build the schedule around sleeping altitude, rest days and the people in your group.

The CDC’s high-altitude guidance recommends limiting increases in sleeping altitude above 3,000 metres to around 500 metres per night, with an additional acclimatisation night for each 1,000 metres gained. Some people need a slower schedule. Fitness doesn’t protect you from altitude illness.

Discuss the larger jumps in any itinerary, including the approach to Namche and the move towards Dole. An extra night lower down or an extra rest day may be useful. No timetable can guarantee that everyone will acclimatise well.

If symptoms suggest altitude illness, stop ascending and seek assessment. Worsening symptoms despite rest call for descent; confusion, difficulty walking normally or breathlessness at rest require urgent help. Discuss prevention, existing conditions and any medication with a clinician familiar with altitude before travelling.

The lesson from our family trip is simple: a missed viewpoint or pass is an acceptable change of plan. Your health should determine the next day’s route.

Do you need a guide and porters?

For this combination of Gokyo, glacier crossings, high passes and an optional climb, I would definitely choose a guide again. It will help you and you support locals.

We travelled with Seven Summit Treks. Our group consisted of six guests, three porters, our guide Angkaji and Temba, who joined us through a longstanding friendship with my father.

My father had met Temba on an earlier Nepal trip. Having him with us made this journey feel much more personal. He had previously worked as a guide and, by the time of our visit, was cooking for expeditions at Everest Base Camp during the climbing season.

The company organised our trek from Kathmandu, including accommodation and meals. We booked our international flights separately. I appreciated having local help with arrangements and changes along the way.

When comparing a similar trip, ask about the actual team and itinerary, including:

  • Experience on Cho La and Kongma La in the expected conditions.
  • Separate climbing experience and arrangements if you add Pokalde.
  • Flexibility when someone needs another rest day or a lower route.
  • Communication and evacuation plans for the quieter sections.
  • Porter loads, equipment, insurance and working conditions.

Our porter allowance was generous, but I would pack well below the maximum. Having help with luggage made the walking more manageable, particularly as a mixed-age group. It also made me very aware of how much work the support team was doing. Don’t forget to bring some extra dollars to give tips after your trip.

What about guide rules and permits?

For planning, check Sagarmatha National Park entry, the Khumbu local Trek-Card, and the requirements applying to your route and guide arrangements.

At the time of research, the Nepal Tourism Board’s park-fee table listed NPR 3,000 for foreign visitors to Sagarmatha National Park outside the Nepali and SAARC categories. The Khumbu municipality separately listed its Trek-Card at NPR 3,000 for visitors outside its SAARC/China category.

The Tourism Board’s TIMS guidance also lists Everest routes under its licensed-guide and agency-issued TIMS provisions. Confirm the current documentation with your registered operator and the relevant authorities for your travel dates; do not assume that one of these cards covers every requirement.

Pokalde needs an additional discussion about climbing arrangements. I will cover the distinction between a peak fee and other paperwork in my upcoming climbing article.

What are the teahouses and food like on the Everest Base Camp trek?

Most nights, we had simple private rooms, usually shared by two people. That suited us well. The cold wasn’t a big issue because we had proper sleeping bags. Thin walls though don’t protect from snoring people in other rooms. Don’t forget earplugs. Namche and Lukla offered more comfortable hotel options, but I would keep expectations flexible farther up the valleys.

What can you eat on the trek?

The food was surprisingly good and much more varied than I had expected. I had imagined a lot of fried rice, but at lunch and dinner we usually had at least five options. The familiar choices repeated between lodges: dal bhat, fried rice, chow mein, noodle soups such as thukpa, momos, spring rolls and several potato dishes, including hash browns. Dal bhat gives you rice, lentil soup and accompanying vegetables or curry; momos are dumplings, with fillings that depend on the kitchen. There were also vegetable, egg and chicken options, plus Western dishes such as pasta, pizza, sandwiches and burgers. I recommend though local options as they are much more tasty. You can eat well on a teahouse trek, although a long printed menu doesn’t mean every lodge has every ingredient available.

I enjoyed the food for most of the journey, then towards the end I suddenly couldn’t face fried dishes, even fried eggs for breakfast. Simpler rice dishes and dal soup became much more appealing. My advice is to choose food you can comfortably eat when tired, rather than force the richest dish on the menu. Bring a small reserve of snacks you know you enjoy, and agree on a water-treatment and refilling plan with your operator.

How do bucket showers work?

Higher up, a “hot shower” can mean a bucket of hot water, some cold water and a small jug. Mix some hot and cold water to a comfortable temperature, use the jug to wet yourself, wash, then pour more water over yourself to rinse. You have a limited amount to work with, so it is a much quicker wash than standing under running water at home. Some lodges have plumbed showers, particularly lower down; the bucket arrangement was part of our mountain experience. Hot water and showers commonly cost extra, so ask the lodge about the price and what is provided, and check if your trekking package includes them. A bucket wash can feel wonderfully luxurious after several cold, dusty days.

Is there internet on the Everest Base Camp trek?

I used Everest Link during our 2022 trek, and it is still a service to ask about: its current website advertises prepaid Wi-Fi cards sold in lodges and shops, with its coverage information listing places along both the main Everest and Gokyo routes. Ask your lodge which connection is working, how to log in, and whether the package is limited by data or validity period before you pay. Availability at a village doesn’t guarantee a reliable connection in every room or while walking between settlements, and I wouldn’t plan important work calls around it. I used it mostly for downloading weather forecasts and letting the world know that I am alive and very happy to share the beauty with them.

Download maps and essential documents beforehand, and use any connection you find for a quick message home. Then put the phone away for a while: this is a wonderful place to spend more time looking at the mountains than at a screen.

My charging tip: a solar panel

I took a solar panel and found it useful for keeping camera equipment and phones charged. We still paid for charging occasionally when the weather didn’t cooperate but we saved pretty a lot of money by having a solar panel.

For a photography trip, I would consider the same arrangement again, alongside a power bank. Keep your essential navigation and communication power separate from the amount you hope to generate during a sunny afternoon.

What should you pack?

For the trekking circuit, I would prioritise multiple layers rather than just bulky warm layers, a suitable sleeping bag and proper hiking shoes that remain comfortable after many consecutive days.

  • Clothing: base layers, second layer, a warm insulating jacket, waterproof jacket and trousers, hat, sun hat and gloves suitable for cold mornings. It gets surprisingly warm during the days but nights and mornings are very cold.
  • Feet: Proper hiking shoes, appropriate socks and a practical blister kit are a must for hiking, don’t forget flip flops for inside
  • Sleeping: a bag with a comfort rating suited to the conditions and the coldest nights (a comfort rating of approximately −10°C, adjusted to the forecast), plus dry clothing to sleep in.
  • Walking: trekking poles, a headtorch and spare power, glacier sunglasses with good side coverage and sun protection.
  • Water and food: reusable bottles, an agreed treatment method and a small reserve of snacks.
  • Electronics: offline maps, a power bank, spare camera batteries and charging cables. Consider a solar panel if you will use it enough to justify the weight.
  • Pass equipment: traction and any additional equipment agreed with your guide for the actual snow and ice conditions.

Try the important equipment before the trip. A Himalayan village is an inconvenient place to discover that your boots and feet have very different plans. In case something breaks, you can buy new equipment in Namche, but not higher up.

Pokalde needs a separate climbing kit. We used crampons, helmets, trekking poles, harnesses and a rope, with protection needed on a short steep section. Those details belong in the climbing briefing as well as on the packing list.

Check that your insurance explicitly covers your planned elevations and activities, including the roped Pokalde climb if you add it, and the rescue arrangements you may need.

Explore Kathmandu after the trek

Plan your main Kathmandu sightseeing after returning from the mountains. Before the trek, allow time to rest, meet the team, check equipment and make the transfer needed for your Lukla flight. Put the extra sightseeing days at the end, where you can use them as a buffer.

Joanne and I landed on 1 October, while my parents arrived two days later. We had plenty of time before the trek, but were more stressed about the return. Next time, I would move more of that spare time to the end.

If you return on schedule, enjoy Swayambhunath, Boudhanath and the historic centre. We enjoyed those places, including a memorable sunrise visit to Swayambhunath. If the mountain journey is delayed, shorten the sightseeing and concentrate on getting back in time for your international flight. I would gladly sacrifice a city day for that flexibility.

My most useful tips after doing this trek

  • Keep the second Namche night. I would simplify an optional extension before removing an acclimatisation day.
  • Allow one more flexible mountain day. Use it for a third Namche night if needed, or more time in Phortse if the trek runs smoothly.
  • Stay in Dzongla. It was one of the places I most enjoyed photographing.
  • Go outside before breakfast. You may still be in shadow while the mountains above you are already glowing.
  • Keep Phortse and a proper descent. My parents loved the village and its gompa. Our shortcut hurt Joanne’s legs and missed that experience.
  • Save Kathmandu sightseeing for afterwards. Those days can become your return-flight buffer.
  • Agree on changes before they become urgent. A plan for a delayed flight or a tired group member makes decisions easier.
  • Look beyond the famous marker. My strongest memories came from viewpoints, villages and a small lakeside camp.
