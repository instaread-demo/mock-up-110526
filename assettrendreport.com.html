Will Rising Data-Center Electricity Demand Actually Benefit Utility Shareholders?

More electricity demand can help regulated utilities, but customer commitments, regulatory recovery, financing costs, and new shares determine how much growth reaches investors.

Yes, but more electric demand only helps utility shareholders when customer payments support recoverable investment and enough earnings survive the financing bill and any new share issuance. I’d rather own a well-funded expansion than a great load forecast that leaves shareholders paying the difference. An allowed return gives the utility an opportunity to earn money, not a guarantee.[6][7]

If you’re looking at utilities as a way to invest in AI, that funding gap deserves your attention. Construction can happen before customer bills arrive. Defensive labels don’t pay for a substation.

A connection request isn’t a paying customer

Different lines of inquiry and different agreements tell different stories: Interests to connect, obligations for preparatory work, terms of service, and service actually connected. Even if fully energized, capacity doesn’t mean the customer will fully use it. The obligations of the agreement are more important than the acronym.[3][5]

AEP’s October 2026 presentation illustrates this. Management said they have 84 GW of incremental contracted load through 2031. Of that, they said they had 35 GW of contracted load in PJM/SPP, 3 GW of contracted load in PJM/SPP with letters of agreement, and 46 GW in letters of agreement in ERCOT. That’s management’s total, not what’s actually energized, not all AI data centers, and not one uniform package of customer protections.[5]

Picture me, in an imagined example, copying a huge load number into a spreadsheet, then getting stuck because the slide’s footnote splits it among different agreements. I get mildly annoyed. The headline took only a few seconds to understand, but the economic meaning takes much longer to decipher. I leave the earnings cell blank until I can connect those agreements to billing dates and payment obligations. An incorrect guess is worse than no guess at all.

What the customer owes if plans change

Ohio Power’s data center tariff illustrates what a meaningful customer protection provision looks like. It does not set a precedent for every AEP subsidiary. Ohio regulators announced their approval on July 9, 2025, and the tariff became effective on July 23. The obligations described below, are shown in a later version of the tariff book (dated August 14, 2025) and on AEP Ohio’s implementation page. Those sources do not necessarily state that all of the legal challenges have been finally decided.[1][2][3]

  • Before service: AEP Ohio describes a reimbursement of 100% of buildout costs if the customer cancels or causes a delay exceeding 12 months.
  • During ramp-up: billing demand is at least 85% of the agreed ramp capacity. The ramp-capacity minima are 50%, 65%, 80%, and 90% of full capacity in years one through four. Post ramp billing uses a different minimum demand formula.
  • For payment security: collateral is set at 50% of full-term minimum charges, unless the credit and liquidity tests are satisfied.
  • For an eligible early exit: after the fifth post ramp year, the described termination payment is 36 months of minimum charges. The contract term combines the ramp period and 8 years.

[2][3]

This is why I resist the shorthand “85% for 12 years.” During the ramp-up, 85% of agreed ramp capacity is not automatically read down to full capacity. Minimum demand billing charges capacity that has been made available; it is not an obligation to purchase the equivalent capacity of electricity. The eligibility rules of the tariff as well as grandfathering and the actual contract terms also affect this.[2]

These terms and conditions provide some flexibility, but they do not provide interlocking assurances. Reimbursement addresses stranded costs; minimum bills address under-use; collateral addresses credit; and exit payments address a defined departure. Consider the costs remaining after those have been provided for. The remainder must be apportioned among the customer, the other customers, and the shareholders, in accordance with the terms of the contracts and the regulations. Missing terms do not mean that this allocation will necessarily be detrimental or beneficial to shareholders.[2][3]

The construction budget isn’t the earning asset base

The amount that’s important is the approved net investment earning a regulated return, not the construction budget. Customer contributions and depreciation can reduce that rate base; in-service dates can impact when an earnings return is recovered; and regulatory decisions can impact what is earning a return. Work with the operating utility and its regulator, because parent-company spending totals won’t provide these answers.[4][6]

In AEP’s third quarter 2026 filing, $100.4 billion of capital expenditures for the years 2027 to 2031 was projected, to be funded from the utility’s cash flows and finance activities. This serves multiple purposes, not all of it is data center spending, or approved rate base. Besides this headline, I’d focus on the $5.2 billion of incremental growth equity, as part of AEP’s presentation. You need both of these numbers to assess the utility’s growth.[4][5]

Construction financing costs can be recognized in the accounts before the cash is provided by customers. If these costs meet the criteria for recovery, they are capitalized. In the case of FERC, accrual of costs is limited during interrupted construction, and ceases when a facility is ready for service. Just because this treatment is allowed, doesn’t mean state commission recovery will be the same.[8]

An allowed return still has to be earned

An authorized return on equity of 10% doesn’t guarantee a 10% shareholder return on the whole rate base. Regulators integrate approved debt and equity proportions with their respective financing costs. They compare allowed and earned equity returns on the same equity base and examine the debt cost recovery separately. Delayed recovery, unrecovered expenses, parent company costs, and other factors can adversely impact common shareholders’ returns.[6][7]

Even covering those costs isn’t enough for me. An authorized return on equity doesn’t resolve the risks investors take. Earned equity returns must justify the risks. Earnings growth by itself doesn’t justify that return.

The result belongs to each share

Let’s do a fully hypothetical analysis. Begin with $500 million of common earnings and 100 million shares, which would result in a $5 EPS. Add $1 billion of approved net rate base, which is financed equally with debt and equity. With a 10% earned equity return and debt costs fully recovered, incremental common earnings would be $50 million, not $100 million.

Assume the entire $500 million equity requirement is newly issued, with investment and issuance effective for a full year. There’s no retained-earnings funding, issuance expense, or other business change. The third case includes an additional $10 million of after-tax unrecovered financing or parent-cost drag. It does not recoup the debt expense.

Hypothetical outcomes from the same $1 billion rate-base addition; starting EPS is $5.
Funding case New shares Total common earnings Final EPS EPS change
Issue equity at $100 5 million $550 million $5.24 +4.8%
Issue equity at $50 10 million $550 million $5.00 0%
Issue at $50; add $10 million cost drag 10 million $540 million $4.91 , 1.8%

For the first case, the arithmetic is $550 million/105 million shares. In the second case it is $550 million/110 million shares. The investments and returns from operations haven't changed. The number of claims to those returns has changed. That is why, in some instances, rate base and earnings per share (EPS) can increase at a faster rate than returns generated by a business.

What would earn more investment credit

I’d feel more comfortable raising my per share estimate as signed commitments turn to more credible service dates and the customer security level keeps pace with the construction exposure. Approved spending and earned returns also support the funding plan. Those are separate judgements. Protective cancellation on equity financings does not fix an expensive financing.

A series of delays in energization, a step-up in spending before customer commitment, and a large equity raise would decrease my per share estimates. Even a large improvement in the business case, price it separately. A stock traded for strong growth can easily disappoint on good but not great overall results.
