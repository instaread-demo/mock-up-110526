How Can I Turn Worked Examples Into Independent Problem-Solving Skill?

By Sophia Bennett
October 27, 2026
6 minute read

When a solution seems obvious but a fresh problem leaves you blank, the missing piece is usually not another careful reread. The example was supplying interpretation, method choice, next moves, and checks, while you were recognizing each choice after it appeared, so independent skill begins when you take those decisions back one at a time.

I used to put a neat check mark beside an example once every line made sense, then feel oddly betrayed by the next exercise. The check mark was not false, but it meant “I can follow this,” not yet “I can start this,” and confusing those two is an annoyingly easy way to study.

A worked example is carrying more decisions than it looks like

Worked examples are especially useful while a method is new because they reduce how much you must generate at once, but their smoothness hides four substantial choices: identifying the goal and relevant information, selecting a principle or strategy, choosing the next operation or representation, and checking whether the result is plausible. Recognizing a formula after it appears is easier than deciding that the formula fits an unfamiliar prompt.

Instead of rereading the entire solution more carefully, pause wherever the solver could reasonably have chosen another route. Those choice points, not every algebraic simplification or line of syntax, are the parts you eventually need to supply yourself.

Study the example at its choice points, not as a script

You do not need to narrate every symbol as though recording an unusually dull audiobook. Annotate only the decisions that reveal why the solution works, using four questions:

  • What is the problem asking me to find, produce, or prove?
  • What clue makes this method fit better than another one?
  • Why is the next meaningful move valid here?
  • What change would make this method fail or become less useful?

The revealing transitions are where the solver chooses an equation setup, formula, diagram, data structure, conditional branch, or algorithm, so cover the solution at one or two of those points and predict the missing choice. A useful explanation names a principle, condition, or goal rather than translating notation into spoken words, and an unexplained choice should remain marked as a stop point instead of disappearing under more finished work.

Use a handoff sequence instead of jumping to a blank page

I find the usual leap from “watch the expert” to “now solve it alone” needlessly punishing. Remove support in stages, so each attempt asks you to take over a little more of the reasoning without piling every difficulty into the same moment.

  1. Study one complete example and label its consequential decisions, including how the goal was interpreted, why the method fit, and how the answer was checked.
  2. Hide the next meaningful step and predict it before looking. If your prediction differs, state whether you missed a clue, a principle, or a procedural move.
  3. Complete a faded problem from the same family. Leave blanks at the setup, formula choice, transformation, or conditional branch, not at boilerplate work that makes the page look incomplete without testing much.
  4. Solve a near-transfer problem with the same underlying structure but changed numbers, wording, context, or layout. Start without notes, then reopen the example only to investigate a specific point where you stopped.
  5. Add a contrasting or mixed problem after you can complete the near-transfer task. The challenge now is deciding which method applies, rather than merely executing a method the worksheet has already announced.

You’re ready for less support when you can supply the current missing move and explain why it applies, but if you’re guessing at the earliest hidden decision or the remaining work collapses, restore that support. Proofs, modeling, design, and programming may not have one tidy route, so fade choices such as framing the problem, selecting a representation, or choosing a test case rather than treating one model solution as the only legitimate path.

Choose follow-up problems by what they ask you to decide

I’m not a fan of practice sets that change the story and numbers while leaving every real decision untouched, because the pages look varied but the thinking stays comfortably identical. The better follow-up targets the decision you still need to practice.

Three follow-up problems that remove different kinds of support
Problem type What changes What it tests
Same-structure faded Keep the method cue, remove a key decision Reconstructing the solution
Near-transfer Change surface details, preserve the core relation Mapping structure to a new prompt
Contrasting or mixed Include a plausible problem requiring another method Choosing a strategy without a label

In programming, you might change the story while preserving the control-flow pattern, while in physics or economics you might change the context but retain the governing relationship. Pair that problem with a similar-looking one requiring a different method, because a set in which every exercise advertises its method or closely resembles the model rehearses execution without testing independent selection.

Let the kind of wrong answer choose the repair

I get frustrated with the advice to “do more problems” because it treats every failure as a shortage of volume. An unaided attempt becomes useful only when the next task targets what broke.

Match the visible failure to the smallest useful repair
Observable pattern Likely gap Next repair
Cannot explain a quantity, rule, condition, output, or why the method applies Conceptual Use a smaller example, state the principle in one sentence, then retry
Method choice is sound, but algebra, units, syntax, notation, or ordering breaks Procedural Drill that move briefly, then use it in one full problem
Can perform familiar methods but chooses by resemblance or cannot select an opening move Strategic Compare two similar-looking problems that need different methods, and write each choice cue first

One wrong answer can contain several problems, and occasionally the prompt or answer key is the unreliable part, so use these categories to choose a repair rather than label your ability. The useful move today is small: choose one recent worked example, cover its earliest consequential choice, supply and explain that move, and solve one near-transfer problem, because collecting more solutions is often just a very organized way to postpone the handoff.
