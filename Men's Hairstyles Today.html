The pompadour is a men’s haircut defined by height at the front, with the hair swept upward and back off the forehead into a rounded rise that holds its shape through the day. It has outlasted nearly every style that shared a decade with it, moving from Louis XV’s court to Elvis to the modern barbershop without ever fully going away.

The most popular pompadour hairstyles are typically textured and lifted at the front with skin fades, low tapers, undercuts and beards, and the finish runs anywhere from wet-look formal to matte and tousled. The cut flatters strong features, gives fine hair a reason to stand up, and scales from a boardroom to a Friday night with a change of product.

In this guide, I’ll walk through 50 of my favorite pompadour hairstyles that pair stylish dimension, classic height and modern flow. I’ll also explain how to ask a barber for the one you actually want, and then cover the blow-dry technique that decides whether the height survives past lunch. Lastly, let’s discuss what to weigh before committing and share the questions I fielded most often behind the chair.

Trendy Pompadour Haircuts

Classic Pompadour Look

The classic pompadour look keeps the front combed up and rolled back into the rounded rise the style is named for, with the sides slicked or tapered close beneath it. Height at the forehead is the whole point, and the finish carries enough shine to read deliberate rather than casual. Everything sits where it was placed. Build the roll with a round brush and dryer, then set it with a medium to firm pomade carrying natural shine.

Low Taper Fade Pompadour

The low taper fade pompadour keeps the graduation close to the hairline, shortening gradually just above the ears and around the neckline while the front carries the height. The subtle perimeter preserves fullness through the sides, so the cut never looks top-heavy, and it grows out more gently than any higher fade. Four or five weeks between visits is realistic here. Comb the front up and back with a medium-hold pomade and let the taper do the tidying.

Modern Pompadour with Low Taper Fade

The modern pompadour with low taper fade pairs a lower, more relaxed front with a clean taper at the edges, trading the 1950s roll for something easier to wear daily. The reduced height cuts styling time noticeably and the matte finish keeps it current. This is the version that suits most guys walking into a shop asking for a pomp. Lift the front with a dryer, then finish with a matte clay rather than anything glossy.

Textured Pompadour

The textured pompadour breaks the front into separated, piece-y sections instead of rolling it into one smooth mass, which is what keeps the height from reading retro. Point-cutting through the top creates that separation, so the finish depends on the cut as much as the product. Tapered or faded sides both work beneath it. Blow-dry the front up and back, then work a matte clay through in small pinches to keep the pieces distinct.

Long Pompadour Fade

The long pompadour fade runs the top well past standard length, producing a fuller rise with genuine movement above cleanly blended sides. The extra length gives the roll somewhere to go and makes the contrast against the fade more dramatic from the side. It also demands healthy hair and a daily dryer. Roll the length back over a round brush and hold it with a firm pomade, then book the fade every two to three weeks.

Combed Over Pompadour

The combed over pompadour directs the front up and diagonally across rather than straight back, lowering the profile and adding a cleaner, more structured line. The reduced height makes it the most conservative version in this family, comfortable in formal settings where a tall pomp would announce itself. A defined part sharpens it further. Comb the top across while damp and finish with a natural-shine pomade for a smooth, controlled result.

Classic Pompadour with Tapered Sides

The classic pompadour with tapered sides pairs a lifted, rounded front with edges shortened gradually around the ears and neckline. The taper keeps the perimeter tidy without pulling attention from the height, and the gradual blend grows out cleanly over four or five weeks. Blow-dry the front up and back over a round brush, then set the shape with a medium-hold pomade.

Long Pompadour with Mid Drop Fade

The long pompadour with mid drop fade carries extra length up top while the blend curves lower behind the ears, following the head’s natural line. That dropped arc keeps weight through the upper back where a straight fade would strip it, and the contrast against the height reads deliberate from every angle. Build the front with a dryer, then hold it with a firm pomade and book the fade every three weeks.

Short Pompadour with Mid Fade

The short pompadour with mid fade trims the top to a modest rise, with the blend starting at the temples for balanced contrast. The reduced length cuts styling time to under a minute and holds through wind and long days without collapsing. It suits guys testing the shape before committing to a taller version. A fingertip of matte clay pushed up and back is the whole routine.

Modern Pompadour with Thick Fringe and Mid Taper

The modern pompadour with thick fringe and mid taper leaves weight at the front hairline so the lift reads full rather than thin, with the taper cleaning the sides at temple height. The heavier fringe softens the whole cut, which is why it wears well on younger guys and anyone who finds a polished pomp too formal. Work a texturizing paste through the front and lift with your fingers rather than a comb.

Parted Pompadour with Taper

The parted pompadour with taper introduces a defined split beside the lifted front, adding structure the pure brushed-back version skips. The part can sit natural or be shaved in as a hard line, and either choice sharpens the cut noticeably in photographs. Short facial hair completes the balance. Set the part with a fine comb while the hair is damp, then finish with a natural-shine pomade.

Pompadour Undercut

The pompadour undercut clips the sides to a single short length with no blend, so the long top sits above a hard line of separation. That disconnection makes the height the entire statement and gives the cut its bolder, more graphic read. It photographs well and grows out with a visible ledge, so plan the appointments. Keep the top lifted with a firm-hold product; anything light lets it sag into the gap.

Classy Pompadour with Short Sides

The classy pompadour with short sides keeps the perimeter scissor-cut rather than clippered, which leaves a softer edge and a more traditional finish. Longer length up top gives the sweep somewhere to go, and thicker hair carries the shape with less help from product. Scissor work also stretches the time between trims. Comb it back while damp and set with a low-shine cream for a finish that reads grown-up rather than styled.

High-Volume Pompadour with Disconnected Undercut Fade

The high-volume pompadour with disconnected undercut fade pushes the front as tall as the hair will hold, with the sides dropping away sharply beneath it. The gap between the two lengths exaggerates the height, so the cut reads dramatic even before styling. This is a commitment to the dryer every morning. Build the lift with a round brush and lock it with a strong pomade, then finish with a light spray if your hair fights gravity.

Brushed Up Pompadour with Short Sides

The brushed up pompadour with short sides sends the top straight up rather than rolling it back, producing a looser, more contemporary lift over buzzed edges. The vertical direction reads younger and takes less precision than a rolled pomp. Fingers do the work here. Push the hair upward while it dries, then run a matte pomade through for hold that stays touchable.

Height and roundness at the forehead define it, and every other entry on this list is a variation on that idea. In my years behind the chair, this was the cut clients asked for by name more than any other retro style. A high-shine, strong-hold pomade gives it the period-correct finish.

The diagonal softens the cut and gives it a retro, unforced quality, while the short tapered sides keep everything anchored. Sweep the hair across while damp with a comb, then hold the angle with a firm-hold product and leave the loose strands alone.

Waves also hold height better than straight hair, which means less product for the same result. A full beard balances the weight up top. Comb the front up and back with a medium-hold pomade and let the taper handle the tidiness.

Everything about it is period-specific, which is the appeal for guys who want the reference rather than a modern interpretation. Nothing subtle survives here. The hard side part pompadour cuts a shaved line beside the lifted top, creating a razor-defined boundary between the height and the shorter side. What Is A Pompadour?

A pompadour is a men’s hairstyle where the longer hair at the front is brushed upward and back to create height and a rounded rise above the forehead, with the sides and back kept shorter through slicking, tapering or fading. The height at the front is the defining feature; everything else is negotiable.

The name traces back to Madame de Pompadour, mistress to King Louis XV of France, whose 18th-century hairstyle piled height above the forehead in a similar way. The men’s version arrived much later and found its defining moment in the 1950s, when Elvis Presley and the rockabilly scene made it the haircut of a generation. It has cycled through revivals ever since without ever disappearing entirely.

The modern pompadour differs from its ancestors mainly below the hairline. Where the traditional version slicked the sides back into a taper, today’s interpretations pair the lifted front with fades, undercuts and beards, and the finish leans matte and textured more often than glossy. The front still goes up and back. Everything else has moved on.

Pompadour vs. Quiff vs. Slick Back

These three styles get used interchangeably, and they shouldn’t be. The distinction is worth knowing before you sit in the chair, because asking for the wrong name is the most common reason guys leave with a cut they didn’t picture.

A pompadour lifts the hair up and rolls it back, with the height concentrated at the front hairline and the shape rounded rather than flat. The volume sits forward.

A quiff also lifts at the front, but the hair points up and slightly forward instead of rolling back, and the finish is usually more textured and less structured. Think of it as the pompadour’s looser cousin.

A slick back takes the hair straight back with no lift at all, lying relatively flat against the head. Same direction, no elevation.

The practical difference lands in the styling. A pompadour needs a round brush and a dryer to build the roll; a quiff can be finger-styled; a slick back needs a comb and product but no height. When a client told me “pompadour” and showed me a photo of a quiff, that conversation saved us both a disappointing haircut.

How To Ask Your Barber For A Pompadour

The pompadour has enough variables that a one-word request leaves too much open. These five details cover everything a barber needs.

Say Pompadour, Then Name the Era

Traditional or modern is the first fork in the road. Traditional means slicked sides, a gentleman’s taper and a glossy finish; modern means faded or undercut sides with matte texture on top. Both are pompadours, and they look nothing alike.

Specify the Length at the Front

The front needs at least three to four inches to roll properly, and the taller versions want five or more. Tell your barber the height you’re after and let them leave the length to achieve it. Too short at the front is the single most common reason a pompadour won’t hold its shape.

Choose the Side Treatment

Taper, low fade, mid fade, high fade, skin fade or disconnected undercut. Each sets both the attitude and the appointment calendar. A taper stretches four or five weeks; a skin fade or disconnect wants two to three.

Ask for Length at the Crown

This detail gets skipped constantly. If the crown is cut too short, the pompadour’s roll has nowhere to travel and the back of your head flattens behind the height. Asking for a little extra length through the crown keeps the profile balanced.

Bring a Photo and Be Honest About Mornings

Photos settle the height question faster than any description. And tell your barber how much time you’ll actually spend, because a tall pompadour is a daily blow-dry commitment. A shorter version styled well beats a tall one you never finish.

What To Think About Before Getting A Pompadour

It Needs a Blow-Dryer

There’s no way around this. Product alone will not build the height, no matter how strong the hold. The dryer sets the shape and the product preserves it, and skipping the first step is why most disappointing pompadours end up flat by mid-morning. If a dryer will never enter your routine, the shorter and brushed-up versions are the honest choice.

Your Hair Type Sets the Difficulty

Thick, straight hair builds the classic shape most easily. Wavy hair holds height well with less product. Fine hair can absolutely wear a pompadour and often looks fuller for it, but needs mousse at the roots and a light product to avoid collapse. Very tight curls make a different shape entirely, rounder and softer, which is a legitimate version rather than a failure.

The Height Affects Your Proportions

Adding inches at the front lengthens the face visually. That’s an advantage for rounder faces and a consideration for longer ones, where a lower rise or a drop fade balances things out. Nothing here rules anyone out; it just steers which version suits you.

The Sides Set Your Schedule

The top forgives a late appointment. The sides don’t. Skin fades and disconnects blur within two weeks, tapers hold four or five. Choose the perimeter that matches how often you genuinely book, not how often you intend to.

It Reads as a Deliberate Choice

A pompadour is never accidental, and people register that. That’s usually the point, but if your workplace prizes understatement, the comb over and low taper versions carry the same idea more quietly.

How To Style A Men’s Pompadour

Styling a pompadour comes down to sequence. Get the order right and the whole thing takes three minutes.

Start with clean, towel-dried hair. Damp, not wet, and definitely not dry.

  1. Apply a pre-styler first if you need volume. A mousse or sea salt spray at the roots gives fine and medium hair something to hold onto before any heavy product goes in.
  2. Blow-dry on medium heat, working against the direction the hair naturally falls. This is the step that builds the shape.
  3. Use a round brush on the front sections, rolling the hair up and back toward the crown. That rolling motion is what creates the pompadour’s signature roundness rather than a flat brush-up.
  4. Hit the front with the dryer’s cool setting for ten seconds while the shape is still on the brush. The temperature drop sets the form and buys you hours of extra hold.
  5. Apply your finishing product last, working it through with your palms and comb rather than dumping it at the roots. Pomade for shine and structure, clay for a fuller matte finish, putty for flexible hold, gel for the old-school wet look.

After fifteen years of watching clients try to skip step four, I’ll say it plainly: the cool shot is the cheapest hold you’ll ever get.

Best Products For A Pompadour

Pomade

The traditional choice, offering hold with shine. Water-based formulas rinse out easily and restyle with damp hands through the day; oil-based ones hold longer but need a proper wash. Medium to firm hold suits most pompadours.

Matte Clay

The modern default, delivering strong hold with no shine and a fuller appearance. Clay suits the textured, messy and fade-paired versions, and it works particularly well on finer hair where gloss would expose the scalp.

Volumizing Mousse

The step most guys skip. Applied to damp roots before drying, mousse builds lift that product alone cannot manufacture. Essential for fine and medium hair, useful for everyone.

Texture Powder or Spray

For separation and grip in the tousled and textured versions. A light dusting at the roots revives a collapsed pomp midday without adding weight.

Light Hairspray

Optional insurance for tall pompadours and long days. A light mist from a distance holds the shape without turning it rigid. Skip it on the relaxed versions, where movement is the point.

What to leave alone: heavy waxes, which drag the height down, and anything promising extreme shine unless you’re deliberately building a traditional wet-look pomp.

Barber-Approved Tips For A Better Pompadour

Dry Against the Grain

Blowing the hair in the direction it already wants to fall accomplishes nothing. Push against the natural fall while drying, and the roots stand up on their own before any product touches them.

Product Goes on Dry Hair, Not Damp

For most finishes, applying pomade or clay after the hair is fully dry gives cleaner hold and better texture. The exception is the traditional wet-look pompadour, which is applied to damp hair by design. Getting this backwards is why some guys find their pomp goes limp.

Warm the Product First

Rub it between your palms until it disappears before it reaches your hair. Cold product applied in a lump creates clumps and uneven hold, which shows immediately in a style built on smooth height.

Ask for Weight Removal Through the Top

Thick hair fights the roll, sitting heavy instead of lifting. Point-cutting through the interior of the top section lightens it enough to rise properly without losing the length you need.

Time Your Cut for the Event

A pompadour styles best about a week after the cut, once the edges have settled and the top has relaxed slightly. Booking the day before a wedding or a shoot rarely produces your best hair.

Maintenance

How Often To Get It Cut

Skin fades and disconnected undercuts need refreshing every two to three weeks. Mid and low fades run three to four. Tapers and scissor-cut sides stretch to four or five. The top itself wants a shaping trim every five to six weeks to hold the front-to-crown graduation that makes the roll possible.

Washing and Care

Daily product means an honest wash routine. Rinse thoroughly at night, shampoo every two or three days with something that clears buildup without stripping, and condition to offset the daily heat. Oil-based pomade users should add a clarifying wash weekly.

Heat Protection

A daily blow-dry adds up over months. A light heat protectant before drying costs five seconds and preserves the condition of the exact hair the style displays most.

The Midday Rescue

A collapsed pompadour revives with damp palms pushed up and back through the front, no additional product. Water reactivates most water-based pomades instantly, which is a good argument for choosing them over oil-based formulas if your days run long.

Popular Men’s Pompadour Hairstyles

The most requested pompadour is the trendy, modern version: faded or tapered sides, naturally textured hair on top, and a front swept up and back into a lower, more relaxed rise than the classic. The reduced height is why it dominates. This variation reads current rather than retro, survives a day of meetings and takes half the styling time of a full 1950s pomp.

Beyond that baseline, the popular variations add a single distinguishing element: a hard part for structure, loose strands for softness, deliberate messiness for ease, or a fluffy finish for lightness. Each is a small adjustment to the same architecture, which is the quiet strength of this cut. You can wear it for years and never wear the same version twice.

Frequently Asked Questions About Pompadours

What is a pompadour haircut?

A pompadour is a men’s hairstyle where the longer front hair is brushed upward and back into a rounded rise above the forehead, with shorter sides and back created through slicking, tapering or fading. The height at the front is what defines it.

How long does hair need to be for a pompadour?

Three to four inches at the front is the practical minimum for a proper roll, with taller versions wanting five inches or more. The sides can be as short as you like. Growing from a short cut usually takes three to five months to reach workable length.

What’s the difference between a pompadour and a quiff?

A pompadour rolls the hair up and back, with the height concentrated at the front and the shape rounded. A quiff points the front up and slightly forward with a looser, more textured finish. The pompadour is the more structured of the two and generally needs a round brush to build.

Can you get a pompadour with thin hair?

Yes, and it’s frequently a flattering choice. The upward sweep creates the impression of density that a flat style can’t, and the shorter sides reduce the contrast that makes thinning visible. Use volumizing mousse at the roots and finish with a matte product rather than anything glossy.

Does a pompadour work with curly hair?

It does, in its own version. Curls build height naturally, so the shape comes together with less heat, though the result is rounder and softer than the straight-haired classic. Define with a curl cream on damp hair and encourage the front upward with your fingers instead of a brush.

How do I keep my pompadour from falling flat?

Blow-dry against the natural fall to build lift at the roots, finish with the cool setting to set the shape, and apply your holding product to dry hair rather than damp. Most collapsed pompadours are dryer problems, not product problems.

Is a pompadour a professional haircut?

The tapered, comb over and low-fade versions read polished in nearly any workplace. The taller skin-faded and disconnected versions lean bolder and suit creative or casual settings better. Height and side contrast are the two dials that move it along that spectrum.

How often should a pompadour be cut?

Every two to three weeks for skin fades and disconnected undercuts, three to four for mid and low fades, and four to five for tapers and scissor-cut sides. The top needs a shaping trim every five to six weeks regardless of the sides.

What product is best for a pompadour?

Pomade for shine and traditional structure, matte clay for modern texture and fuller-looking hair, and volumizing mousse underneath either if your hair is fine. High-shine gel suits the wet-look traditional pomp. Skip heavy waxes, which weigh the height down.

Is the pompadour still in style?

Yes, though the current version looks different from the 1950s original. Modern pompadours favor lower height, matte finishes and faded or undercut sides, which is why they still appear regularly in barbershops rather than only at themed events. The classic is available whenever you want it.
